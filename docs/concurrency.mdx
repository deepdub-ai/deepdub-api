There are two ways to consume the Deepdub TTS API. This page covers both — what each one is, how each is billed, how concurrency actually works, and how to pick (or combine) them.

If you only read one section, read [The key insight](#the-key-insight): one concurrency slot serves several live calls, because a slot processes in-flight requests, not whole calls.

---

## The two ways to consume Deepdub TTS

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Public queue" icon="cloud">
    A shared model pool. Send a request, get audio back. We manage capacity, scaling, and routing — you manage nothing.

    - **You don't manage concurrency.** Every request is just an HTTP/WebSocket call.
    - **Rate-limiting applies, based on your subscription package.** Larger packages have higher limits. If you exceed your limit, you get a `429 RateLimit` error.
    - **Pay per second of audio generated.** 60 seconds of output = 1,000 credits.
    - **Shared with all other customers.** Peaks elsewhere can affect your latency.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Dedicated model (enterprise)" icon="server">
    A private model pool reserved for your account. You request N concurrency, we hold capacity. You decide how much, and when.

    - **You pick the concurrency level.** Scale up before peak, down after — controlled via the API.
    - **No rate limit.** You are bounded only by the concurrency you reserved.
    - **Pay per minute of reserved capacity.** 1 concurrency × 60 minutes = 60,000 credits.
    - **Isolated from public load.** Predictable latency, your own queue.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## The definition

**Concurrency is a commitment, not a call cap.**

Concurrency = N means: at any moment, **N requests can be in flight simultaneously** without waiting.

- If the queue is empty, the next request starts processing immediately — that's the SLA.
- If N+1 requests arrive at exactly the same instant, request N+1 waits until one of the in-flight slots frees up.
- Each slot processes one TTS request at a time. When a slot finishes streaming, it picks up the next message from the queue.

<Warning>
**Concurrency is not the same as "number of calls."** One concurrency slot can comfortably handle several live calls' worth of requests. We'll show why next.
</Warning>

## How a request travels

From your bot to streamed audio in five steps:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Bot sends request">
    Your voice bot sends a TTS request over REST or WebSocket — text, voice prompt ID, locale.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Request lands in the queue">
    If a slot is free, it's picked up instantly. If all N slots are busy, it waits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Slot generates audio">
    A slot generates the audio. Time-to-first-audio: **100–300 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Audio streams back">
    Audio streams to your bot as it's generated — typically faster than the audio's playback length (Real-Time Factor 1.7×–5×).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Slot frees up">
    The slot frees up as soon as generation finishes — **even though your bot is still playing the audio to the caller**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The last step is the foundation of the next section.

## The key insight

**One concurrency slot serves several live calls' worth of requests.**

This is because two things are true about voice-bot traffic that aren't true about, say, a render farm:

### Reason 1 — A call has silence

In a live conversation, the bot only speaks part of the time. While the caller is speaking — or while you're waiting for an LLM to produce the next answer — the model isn't generating anything for that call.

Roughly speaking, the bot speaks somewhere between **20% and 50%** of total call time. The exact ratio depends on the use case (sales call vs. support, monologue vs. interactive). The rest is the caller's turn or LLM wait time — and the model is free for other calls during that window.

### Reason 2 — Generation is faster than playback

A 9-second sentence is generated in roughly 2–5 seconds (Real-Time Factor between **1.7× and 5×**). Once generation finishes, the slot is free even though your bot is still playing the audio to the caller in real time.

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Generation speed (RTF) | 1.7×–5× faster than real-time playback |
| Time to first audio | 100–300 ms |
| Free time per request | A large fraction of audio length |

**Together: a slot is busy generating for only a fraction of a call's duration. The rest of the time it serves other calls.**

Because of these two factors, the commitment we make is per **concurrent in-flight request**, not per active call.

## Worked example — public queue

A representative voice-agent call:

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Average call length | 5 minutes |
| Bot speaks (share of total call) | ~50% |
| Generated audio per call (bot speech) | ~2.5 minutes (150 s) |

**A day on the public queue, 4,000 calls:**

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Calls handled | 4,000 |
| Generated audio per call | 150 s |
| Total generated audio | 600,000 s (10,000 min) |
| Credit rate | 1,000 credits per 60 s |
| **Credits** | **10,000,000** |
| You manage | Nothing — rate-limiting is enforced by your subscription package |

Scaling is automatic. You pay for what the bot actually generates.

## Worked example — dedicated model

Same volume — 4,000 calls — spread over a 10-hour business day.

**Sizing the reservation.** Each call's bot-speech (~2.5 min) needs ~0.5–1.5 min of slot time (RTF 1.7×–5×), plus the rest of the call has silence/LLM-wait windows another slot can fill. With 4,000 calls over 10 hours, you can size to a handful of slots if the calls are evenly distributed, more if they're peaky. Start from peak concurrent in-flight generations measured in your own monitoring, not from peak concurrent calls.

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Calls handled | 4,000 |
| Window | 10 hours (business day) |
| Reserved concurrency | 10 slots (illustrative — tune from your in-flight metric) |
| Hours reserved | 10 |
| Audio generated | Doesn't affect billing |
| Credit rate | 1,000 credits per slot-minute |
| **Credits** | **6,000,000** (10 slots × 600 min × 1,000) |
| You manage | Concurrency level + when to scale |

The flip side of paying for reserved time: at high utilization, you get many more output minutes per credit than the public rate.

## Provisioning — new concurrency takes minutes to come online

Requesting concurrency is a REST call. We then spin up the model's reserved capacity according to your request. Provisioning time depends on demand at that moment — usually a few minutes, sometimes longer for large jumps or first spin-ups.

| Time | What's happening |
| --- | --- |
| t = 0 | You call the concurrency-set endpoint |
| +30 s | Deepdub schedules new model capacity |
| +2–10 min | Models warm up; new slots come online |
| ready | All N slots serve traffic instantly |

<Tip>
**Rule of thumb:** pre-warm capacity at least 5 minutes before your expected peak. For large jumps (10× and up), pre-warm 10–15 minutes ahead.
</Tip>

If you know you'll need concurrency = 100 at 9:00 AM Monday, request it at 8:45 AM. Don't wait for the load to arrive first.

## Side-by-side

|  | Public queue | Dedicated model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who shares it** | All Deepdub customers | Only you |
| **Latency under load** | Can be affected by peaks elsewhere | Predictable — you own the queue |
| **Billing model** | Per second of audio generated | Per minute of reserved slot |
| **Concurrency management** | Rate-limited by your subscription package | You set N via API; pre-warm before peaks |
| **Region availability** | US today; EU not yet live | US and EU available now |
| **Best fit for** | Early-stage, bursty, low-volume, off-peak | Production voice bots, peak hours, latency-critical |

## The hybrid pattern — use both

A common production setup combines them:

- **Business hours →** dedicated model. Predictable latency, sized for your peak.
- **Overnight / low volume →** route to the public queue. No reserved capacity wasted.
- **Overflow →** if dedicated is saturated and a small delay is acceptable, spill to public.

The split is enforced in your code by switching the endpoint and model name per request.

**A typical week:**

| When | Routing |
| --- | --- |
| Mon–Fri, 09:00–17:00 | Dedicated, N = 50 |
| Mon–Fri, 17:00–09:00 | Public queue |
| Sat–Sun, all day | Public queue |

**Result:** predictable latency when it matters, no idle reserved capacity when it doesn't.

## Where it runs

### Regions

- **US:** both public and dedicated available.
- **EU:** dedicated model available now; EU public queue is not yet live.

End-user latency depends on where your bots run, not where your callers are.

### Endpoints and models

- **Public:** `dd-etts-3.2` — current generally-available model. See [Models](/models).
- **Dedicated:** named after your account (e.g., `MyCompany 2`, `MyCompany 4`). Bumps to v3, v4… as we release.
- **Switching:** change the endpoint URL and the `model` parameter per request.

Full URLs and auth parameters live in [Authentication](/authentication) and the [API Reference](/api-reference/tts/generate-and-stream-tts-audio).

## What to build on your side

For dedicated-model operations to work smoothly, instrument these on your end:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="In-flight dashboard">
    Track requests sent, time-of-send, time-of-first-audio, time-of-completion. Plot concurrent in-flight count over time. This is what tells you whether your current concurrency is right-sized.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Pre-warm schedule">
    A cron or scheduler that bumps concurrency 5–15 min before known peaks, then scales down after. Tie this to your business calendar (campaigns, weekday peaks, scheduled releases).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Routing layer">
    A flag per request to choose dedicated vs. public. Default to dedicated; fall back to public when dedicated is saturated or it's off-hours.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Pre-recorded greeting">
    Cache the first sentence of each call as audio. Saves credits, locks in a perfect first impression, and hides any cold-start latency on the very first request of a call.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Save the generationId from each response">
    Log the `generationId` returned per request. It's what we need to debug any issue end-to-end. You can either include one of your own or let Deepdub assign one.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A/B testing harness">
    Same agent, two voices or two prompts. Compare drop-off and call success to tune from real data.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Recap

Six things to take away:

1. **Concurrency is a commitment** to N parallel in-flight generations, not a cap on calls.
2. **One slot serves several calls' worth of requests** because a call has silence (caller's turn, LLM wait) and generation is faster than playback (RTF 1.7×–5×).
3. **Public = pay per second of audio, rate-limited by package**. **Dedicated = pay per minute of reserved slot, no rate limit, customer-controlled concurrency.**
4. **Pre-warm 5–15 minutes before peaks.** Model provisioning is not instant.
5. **Hybrid is the production pattern:** dedicated during business hours, public for the rest.
6. **Instrument in-flight count** on your side and save the `generationId` returned on every request.
