There are two ways to consume the Deepdub TTS API. This page covers both — what each one is, how each is billed, how concurrency actually works, and how to pick (or combine) them.

If you only read one section, read [The key insight](#the-key-insight): one concurrency slot serves several live calls, which is why sizing concurrency to your peak call count overprovisions by 4–6×.

---

## The two ways to consume Deepdub TTS

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Public queue" icon="cloud">
    Shared inference pool. Send a request, get audio back. We manage capacity, scaling, and routing — you manage nothing.

    - **You don't manage concurrency.** Every request is just an HTTP/WebSocket call.
    - **Pay per second of audio generated.** 60 seconds of output = 1,000 credits.
    - **Shared with all other customers.** Peaks elsewhere can affect your latency.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Dedicated model (enterprise)" icon="server">
    A private inference pool reserved for your account. You request N concurrency, we hold capacity. You decide how much, and when.

    - **You pick the concurrency level.** Scale up before peak, down after.
    - **Pay per minute of reserved capacity.** 1 concurrency × 60 minutes = 60,000 credits.
    - **Isolated from public load.** Predictable latency, your own queue.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## The definition

**Concurrency is a commitment, not a call cap.**

Concurrency = N means: at any moment, **N requests can be in flight simultaneously** without waiting.

- If the queue is empty, the next request starts processing immediately — that's the SLA.
- If N+1 requests arrive at exactly the same instant, request N+1 waits until one of the in-flight slots frees up.
- Each slot processes one TTS request at a time. When a slot finishes streaming, it picks up the next message from the queue.

<Warning>
**Concurrency is not the same as "number of calls."** One concurrency slot can comfortably handle several live calls. We'll show why next.
</Warning>

## How a request travels

From your bot to streamed audio in five steps:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Bot sends request">
    Your voice bot sends a TTS request over REST or WebSocket — text, voice ID, prompt, target gender.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Request lands in the queue">
    If a slot is free, it's picked up instantly. If all N slots are busy, it waits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Inference slot generates audio">
    An inference slot generates the audio. Time-to-first-audio: **~200–300 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Audio streams back">
    Audio streams to your bot as it's generated — typically 3× faster than the audio's playback length.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Slot frees up">
    The slot frees up as soon as generation finishes — **even though your bot is still playing the audio to the caller**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The last step is the foundation of the next section.

## The key insight

**One concurrency slot serves several live calls.**

This is because two things are true about voice-bot traffic that aren't true about, say, a render farm:

### Reason 1 — A call has silence

The bot only speaks part of the time. While the caller is speaking, the model isn't generating anything for that call.

In a typical conversation, the bot speaks roughly half the time. The rest is the caller's turn — and the model isn't busy for that call. Across N concurrent calls, the bot's speaking windows are staggered in time; they don't line up at the same millisecond. That stagger is what lets one slot serve multiple calls without any single caller waiting.

### Reason 2 — Generation beats playback

A 9-second sentence is generated in **~3 seconds**. The slot is free during the remaining 6 seconds while your bot streams the audio to the caller in real time.

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Generation speed | 3–5× faster than real-time playback |
| Time to first audio | 200–300 ms |
| Free time per request | ~67% of audio length |

**Together: a slot is busy generating for only a fraction of a call's duration. The rest of the time it can serve other calls.**

## Worked example

### Scenario A — Concurrency = 1, single 9-second sentence

| Phase | Duration |
| --- | --- |
| Generation time | ~3 s |
| Playback time | 9 s |
| Slot free after | 3 s |
| Slot idle for | 6 s (while the caller hears the audio) |

During those 6 idle seconds, the same slot can accept a request from another call.

### Scenario B — Concurrency = 100, how many live calls can you run?

- 100 slots, each busy ~⅓ of the time per call
- Add call-side silence (caller's turn): another ~½ reduction
- **Effective capacity: several hundred concurrent calls**

<Note>
**Concurrency = 100 doesn't mean 100 calls max.** It means at most 100 sentences being generated at the same instant. When sizing, start from **peak concurrent generations**, not peak concurrent calls. We recommend instrumenting your in-flight count (see [What to build on your side](#what-to-build-on-your-side)) and tuning from real data.
</Note>

## Provisioning — new concurrency takes minutes to come online

Requesting concurrency is a REST call. We then spin up GPU instances on AWS to back those slots. Cold-start time depends on AWS capacity at that moment — usually a few minutes, sometimes longer for large jumps.

| Time | What's happening |
| --- | --- |
| t = 0 | You call the concurrency-set endpoint |
| +30 s | Deepdub schedules GPU instances on AWS |
| +2–5 min | Models warm up; new slots come online |
| ready | All N slots serve traffic instantly |

<Tip>
**Rule of thumb:** pre-warm capacity at least 5 minutes before your expected peak. For large jumps (10× and up), pre-warm 15 minutes ahead.
</Tip>

If you know you'll need concurrency = 100 at 9:00 AM Monday, request it at 8:45 AM. Don't wait for the load to arrive first.

## Billing — public queue

Public: pay for every second of audio generated. We meter the **length of the audio output**. That's the only signal — we don't see calls, sessions, or users.

**Rate:** 60 seconds of audio = 1,000 credits. Linear: 30 s = 500 credits, 6 s = 100 credits.

If you generate 10 minutes of speech across a thousand calls, you pay for 10 minutes — regardless of how the requests were distributed.

**Example day · public queue:**

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Calls handled | 4,000 |
| Avg. bot speech / call | 45 seconds |
| Total audio | 180,000 s (3,000 min) |
| **Credits** | **3,000,000** |
| You manage | Nothing — we handle capacity |

---

## Billing — dedicated model

Dedicated: pay for **reserved capacity over time**. When you request concurrency, the clock starts. We bill per minute of reserved slot, whether or not you use it.

**Rate:** 1 concurrency × 1 hour = 60,000 credits (i.e., 1,000 credits per concurrency per minute, regardless of throughput).

The flip side: with high utilization, you get many more output minutes per credit than the public rate.

**Example day · dedicated model:**

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Reserved concurrency | 10 slots |
| Hours reserved | 10 hours (business day) |
| Audio generated | Doesn't affect billing |
| **Credits** | **6,000,000** (10 × 10 × 60,000) |
| You manage | Concurrency level + when to scale |

## Side-by-side

|  | Public queue | Dedicated model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who shares it** | All Deepdub customers | Only you |
| **Latency under load** | Can be affected by peaks elsewhere | Predictable — you own the queue |
| **Billing model** | Per second of audio generated | Per minute of reserved slot |
| **Concurrency management** | None — we handle it | You set N; pre-warm before peaks |
| **Region availability** | US today; EU rolling out | US and EU available now |
| **Best fit for** | Early-stage, bursty, low-volume, off-peak | Production voice bots, peak hours, latency-critical |

## The hybrid pattern — use both

A common production setup combines them:

- **Business hours →** dedicated model. Predictable latency, sized for your peak.
- **Overnight / low volume →** route to the public queue. No reserved capacity wasted.
- **Overflow →** if dedicated is saturated and a small delay is acceptable, spill to public.

The split is enforced in your code by switching the endpoint and model name per request.

**A typical week:**

| When | Routing |
| --- | --- |
| Mon–Fri, 09:00–17:00 | Dedicated, N = 50 |
| Mon–Fri, 17:00–09:00 | Public queue |
| Sat–Sun, all day | Public queue |

**Result:** predictable latency when it matters, no idle reserved capacity when it doesn't.

## Where it runs

### Regions

- **US:** both public and dedicated available.
- **EU:** dedicated model available now; EU public queue rolling out.

End-user latency depends on where your bots run, not where your callers are.

### Endpoints and models

- **Public:** `dd-etts-3.3` — current generally-available model. See [Models](/models).
- **Dedicated:** named after your account (e.g., `MyCompany 2`, `MyCompany 4`). Bumps to v3, v4… as we release.
- **Switching:** change the endpoint URL and the `model` parameter per request.

Full URLs, auth, and CI/CD parameters live in [Authentication](/authentication) and the [API Reference](/api-reference/tts/generate-and-stream-tts-audio).

## What to build on your side

For dedicated-model operations to work smoothly, instrument these on your end:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="In-flight dashboard">
    Track requests sent, time-of-send, time-of-first-audio, time-of-completion. Plot concurrent in-flight count over time. This is what tells you whether your current concurrency is right-sized.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Pre-warm schedule">
    A cron or scheduler that bumps concurrency 5–15 min before known peaks, then scales down after. Tie this to your business calendar (campaigns, weekday peaks, scheduled releases).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Routing layer">
    A flag per request to choose dedicated vs. public. Default to dedicated; fall back to public when dedicated is saturated or it's off-hours.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Pre-recorded greeting">
    Cache the first sentence of each call as audio. Saves credits, locks in a perfect first impression, and hides any cold-start latency on the very first request of a call.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Generation IDs in logs">
    Store the `generationId` returned per request. It's what we need to debug any issue end-to-end.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A/B testing harness">
    Same agent, two voices or two prompts. Compare drop-off and call success to tune from real data.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Recap

Six things to take away:

1. **Concurrency is a commitment** to N parallel in-flight generations, not a cap on calls.
2. **One slot serves several live calls** because a call has silence and generation is 3–5× faster than playback.
3. **Public = pay per second of audio**, no management. **Dedicated = pay per minute of reserved slot**, you manage scale.
4. **Pre-warm 5–15 minutes before peaks.** AWS GPU provisioning is not instant.
5. **Hybrid is the production pattern:** dedicated during business hours, public for the rest.
6. **Instrument in-flight count** on your side and log `generationId` on every request.
